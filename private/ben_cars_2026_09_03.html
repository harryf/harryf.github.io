The Abarth Ben found (85,000 km, CHF 10,000): is it a good deal?

Short answer: it is priced at the very top of the market, and only justified if it is a Supersport 180 with a full oil and belt history and no chip. Nothing at 85,000 km showed up on autoscout24 or tutti today, so it may be on Ricardo or Comparis, or already sold. Either way, here is how to judge it.

What the live market says

  • Under CHF 11,000 there are only about 3 Abarth Puntos on autoscout24 and 5 on tutti in the whole of Switzerland today.
  • Asking prices seen: CHF 7,200 at 173k km, CHF 8,980 at 121k km, CHF 9,000 at 117k km, CHF 10,000 at 142k km (Bern), CHF 10,690 at 109k km (Vaud). Over budget: CHF 11,900 at 99,900 km, CHF 12,500 at 107k km.
  • Rule of thumb from that data: about CHF 7k at 170 to 190k km, CHF 9 to 11k at 100 to 120k km, CHF 12 to 14k under 100k km.
  • So CHF 10,000 for 85,000 km is fair-to-slightly-high, not a bargain. There is CHF 500 to 1,000 of room, more if any of the checks below fails.

Questions that decide it

  • Which engine? 2008 to 2010 Grande Punto Abarth = plain 1.4 T-Jet (155 PS, or 180 PS esseesse kit). 2010 to 2014 Punto Evo Abarth = 1.4 MultiAir Turbo (163 PS, 180 PS Supersport). The T-Jet is the more robust one; the MultiAir needs the right oil, every time.
  • Cam belt and water pump done? Interval 5 years / 100,000 km. At 85k km on a 12-plus-year-old car it is due by age. If no receipt, deduct CHF 800 to 1,200.
  • Chipped? Ask outright and read the ECU. A chipped T-Jet eats its clutch and dual-mass flywheel (CHF 1,200 to 2,000).
  • Clutch and M32 gearbox: hard pull in 5th and 6th, listen for bearing whine, check 5th engages cleanly under load.
  • Rust: rear wheel arches, door and tailgate seams, rear-left corner of the engine bay.
  • MFK date and service book: "frisch ab MFK" within the last few months, stamps from a Fiat or Abarth dealer.
  • 85,000 km in 12+ years is very low. Low-km cars can have perished hoses, seized calipers and a flat-spotted clutch; a low-km car that sat is not better than a 120k km car that was driven and serviced.

Compare directly with A1: the Teufenthal SS is CHF 1,000 cheaper, first Swiss hand, MFK in February, all services documented, and a dealer will add a 12-month engine and gearbox warranty. Unless Ben's car is a Supersport with everything proven, A1 is the better Abarth.

Known problems by model, at 100,000 to 200,000 km

Abarth Punto 1.4 T-Jet and MultiAir

  • Engine itself is strong; owners run to 190k km without oil top-ups. Cam belt and water pump every 5 years / 100k km, plugs every 30k km.
  • Clutch is the number one wear item; dual-mass flywheel fails on chipped cars. M32 gearbox bearings on the Grande Punto (whine, 5th gear).
  • MultiAir (Evo, 2010 to 2014): brick failure after wrong oil, documented at 38k, 44k and 65k km. Use only 9.55535-S2 oil.
  • Turbo: isolated early failures and boost-hose leaks; typical life 100 to 150k km.
  • Rust at door, bonnet and tailgate seams, rear arches, exhaust hangers. Blue&Me can drain the battery; window regulators; weak battery gives odd steering and temperature warnings.
  • Parts: cheap and plentiful (shared with Fiat 500 Abarth). Abarth service partners near Zurich include Schloss-Garage Winterthur, Häusermann Zürich and Herrlig-Garage Uitikon.

Mercedes engines and gearboxes

  • M271 (C 200 K, C 180/200/250 CGI, CLK 200 K, SLK 200 K): simplex timing chain and camshaft adjusters stretch, sometimes under 100k km. Breather hoses clog and oil pools in the intercooler. Cam solenoids leak oil into the harness. CGI: high-pressure pump and carboned intake valves.
  • M272 V6 (C 280/350 pre-facelift, CLK 280/350, SLK 280/350, E 280/350): balance-shaft sprocket on engines built roughly 2005 to early 2007, engine-out repair. Intake manifold swirl-flap arms break at 130 to 160k km. 2008 onward is past the sprocket problem.
  • M276 V6 (C 350 facelift 2011+): no balance-shaft issue; watch oil cooler seals, cam cover leaks and chain noise at high km.
  • OM651 diesel (C 200/220/250 CDI): early piezo injectors fail; 200/220 got better solenoid injectors from 2011, the 250 CDI did not. Chain tensioner and oil-loss recall. DPF needs real driving.
  • 7G-Tronic (722.9): conductor plate fails from 80k km, needs coding after replacement. Change the fluid every 50 to 100k km regardless of what "lifetime" says.
  • 7G-DCT (A/B/CLA/GLA): clutch judder, mechatronics at 120 to 170k km; fluid every 60k km. The only A 250 in region under budget was marked "for export" and left out.
  • W204 body: electric steering lock (ESL) dies at 8 to 12 years; rear subframe rusts from the inside; rust on tailgate and front wings; parking brake seizes. W203 (pre-2007) rusts everywhere; W211 pre-2006 has SBC brakes; both left off this list.

BMW engines and gearboxes

  • N52 (125i, 130i, 325i, 328i, 330i to 2010, and xDrive 330s later): the good one. Electric water pump and thermostat around 130k km, valve cover and oil filter housing gaskets, VANOS solenoids, eccentric shaft sensor seal. No direct injection, no high-pressure pump.
  • N43 (118i/120i/318i/320i 2007 to 2013): chain guides break up and block the oil pick-up; piezo injectors and the NOx sensor fail and need coding. Widely advised to avoid.
  • N53 (325i/330i rear-drive 2007 to 2011): high-pressure pump, leaking injectors, NOx sensor and NOx cat. Worst-quartile reliability.
  • N20 (F20 125i, F30 320i/328i 2012 to 2015): plastic chain guides from bad polymer batches, rattle then skipped chain. Needs proof of replacement.
  • N13 (F20 116i/118i): same chain-guide family problem.
  • N47 diesel (118d/120d/320d 2007 to 2011): chain at the back of the engine, gearbox out to fix, CHF 2,000 to 4,000 preventive. Swirl flaps, DPF. Left off this list.
  • ZF 6HP automatic: mechatronic sleeve and adapter seals leak; pan, filter, sleeve and fluid is a known service.
  • E87/E90 chassis: rear subframe and trailing-arm bushings, brittle cooling plastics, window regulators, tie rods, broken coil springs.
  • Parts cost is comparable to Mercedes; the tool to buy first is an ISTA/INPA laptop, because injectors and many sensors need coding.

Dealer or private seller? What is different in Switzerland

Buying from a dealer (Händler)

  • Garantie: dealers can include or sell an Occasionsgarantie (typically 12 months / 20,000 km, often engine and gearbox only, sometimes via Quality1). Read what it covers; wear items are excluded.
  • Ab MFK / frisch ab MFK: the car passed the cantonal inspection recently. It is a roadworthiness check, not a mechanical guarantee: a car can pass MFK with a stretched timing chain.
  • Frisch ab Service: ask for the invoice showing what was actually done. "Service" can mean an oil change.
  • The Occasionsgarantie is voluntary, not a legal right. Statutory Gewährleistung under the Code of Obligations can be, and usually is, excluded in the contract with "ohne Garantie / wie besichtigt"; that exclusion holds unless the seller actively hid a defect. So the paper matters more than the reputation.
  • Prices are higher, but negotiation room of 5 to 10% is normal, more on cars listed for over 60 days.

Buying private (Privatverkauf)

  • Cheaper, and for a mechanic who can inspect the car himself it is often the better deal: M1 and M3 above are both private and both have better history than the dealer cars.
  • Private sales in Switzerland almost always exclude Gewährleistung in writing ("wie gesehen", "ohne Garantie", Article 199 of the Code of Obligations) and the exclusion is valid unless the seller lied, so the inspection is entirely on him. Bring a scanner (Xentry or a good OBD tool for Mercedes, INPA for BMW, MultiECUScan for Fiat) and read every module.
  • Ask for the Serviceheft (stamps), all invoices, both keys, the Fahrzeugausweis and the last MFK report (Prüfbericht) with any Mängel listed.
  • Payment: bank transfer at the Strassenverkehrsamt or cash against the signed Fahrzeugausweis; never pay a deposit to "hold" a car sight unseen.
  • Check for an open Eintausch or leasing: the Fahrzeugausweis must be in the seller's name and free of a "Code 178" (halter change block).

Checklist before money changes hands

Questions to ask the seller

  • When was the last MFK, and can I see the Prüfbericht?
  • Full service history? Where was it serviced? Can I see the invoices, not just stamps?
  • Timing chain or belt: has it ever been replaced, and when? (Chain on M271, N20, N43; belt on the Abarth.)
  • Automatic gearbox: when was the fluid last changed?
  • Any accident, any repainted panels, any Eintausch or open finance?
  • Is the car chipped or modified, and is every modification entered on the Fahrzeugausweis?
  • Why are you selling, and how long has it been for sale?
  • How many keys, and is the second one coded?
  • Summer and winter wheels included (8-fach bereift)? What condition?

Red flags

  • No service book, or stamps without invoices.
  • Imported car (Typenschein X, "Italienisches Fahrzeug", "Direktimport") with no Swiss history: harder to sell on, and often a hidden-damage history.
  • Tuning, lowering or exhaust mods without a Beiblatt or entry in the Fahrzeugausweis: the MFK will fail it.
  • "Für Export", "Bastler", "wird ab Platz verkauft": project cars, not commuter cars.
  • Fresh paint on one panel, mismatched tyre brands, new underbody coating (hides rust), warning lights that were cleared before you arrived (check freeze-frame data).
  • Dealer with no Google reviews, or a "Probefahrt kostet CHF 60" policy.
  • MFK expired more than a few months ago and the seller "can arrange it for a surcharge".

Running costs for a young driver

  • Insurance is the big one, and it can rule out half this page. Comparis (January 2025, 43,877 quotes) found drivers under 25 pay up to CHF 2,361 a year more between the cheapest and dearest insurer, and 18 to 25 year olds pay roughly three times what a 35-year-old pays for liability. Roughly: liability plus Teilkasko for him is CHF 850 to 2,000 a year on the 100 to 150 kW cars and CHF 1,300 to 2,700 on the 195 to 200 kW cars, before any telematics discount, and Vollkasko on a 265 to 306 PS car (130i, C 350, CLK 350) runs CHF 2,200 to 4,200 if it is offered at all, which on a car worth CHF 7,000 is more than it loses in value. A 184 PS C 200, a 182 PS Fiesta ST, a 180 PS Abarth or a 136 PS Swift Sport will quote far lower. Get real quotes for two or three cars (AXA, Zurich, Mobiliar, Smile) before choosing; every autoscout24 ad has a "Versicherungsprämie berechnen" button. AXA gives a 15% under-26 discount and Mobiliar CleverDrive up to 25% with telematics. Per-car estimates and the probationary-licence rules are in the insurance section below.
  • Teilkasko, not Vollkasko: on a CHF 10,000 car bought with cash the full-cover premium rarely pays for itself. If any of it is financed, the lender will require Vollkasko, which is another reason not to finance a first car.
  • Fuel: Abarth real-world 7 to 8 l/100 km, C 200 K about 8, C 250 CGI about 8, C 250 CDI about 5.5, 130i about 9 to 10, C 350 about 10. On a 40 km round-trip commute the diesel saves roughly CHF 600 a year over a V6.
  • Cantonal road tax (Verkehrsabgabe): each canton has its own formula (engine size, total weight or CO2), and the Zurich Strassenverkehrsamt has an online calculator per model. Heavier estates and the 3.0 and 3.5 litre cars will cost more per year than the 1.4 Abarth or the Swift; the difference is a few hundred francs, not thousands.
  • Tyres: 8-fach bereift (two wheel sets) is worth CHF 800 to 1,500; several cars above include it.
  • Budget for year one: whichever car, put CHF 1,500 aside for the first big job (belt on the Abarth, chain on the M271, water pump on the N52) and do it in the first month rather than waiting for it to fail.

Insurance for Ben: what limits him, and what it will roughly cost

Profile used: 20 years old, canton Zurich, full category B licence for about one year, first car, cash purchase. Sources checked 3 September 2026; figures are estimates derived from published studies, not quotes.

The licence side (law, not insurer policy)

  • He is on a Führerausweis auf Probe until about 2028. A first category B licence is probationary for 3 years. The end date is printed on the licence at field 4b.
  • WAB course: for licences issued after 2019 the one-day, 7-hour Weiterausbildung (WAB 2.0) must be attended within 12 months of the licence date, or a police check means a fine. If he has not done it yet, book it now; Mobiliar pays CHF 100 toward it for their young customers.
  • 0.1 promille alcohol limit for probationary drivers (0.5 for everyone else), which in practice means none. A bare breach without an accident or other offence is graded as a light offence and normally gets a written warning the first time; anything graded medium or serious (higher reading, accident, speeding on top) is a withdrawal, and that triggers the next rule.
  • One withdrawal extends the probation by a year (Art. 15a SVG). A warning or a fine alone does not. A second withdrawal during the probation annuls the licence: a new learner permit only after a 12-month wait and a traffic-psychology assessment, then the test and the WAB course again. Withdrawal-grade offences are speeding well over the limit, alcohol or drugs with aggravating factors, phone-at-the-wheel with endangerment, dangerous overtaking; a parking fine does not count.
  • No legal power limit. Switzerland has no PS or kW restriction for category B on a probationary licence. A 2022 motion to limit young drivers to less powerful cars was rejected by parliament (the Federal Council noted that only 3.6% of serious accidents in 2019 involved new drivers in cars over 136 PS). So the 265 PS 130i is legal for him; whether anyone will insure it at a sane price is a different question.

The insurer side (where the real limits are)

  • He counts as both Junglenker (under 25) and Neulenker (licence under 2 years). Both load the premium, and most insurers add a forced young-driver deductible: the industry default is CHF 1,000 for under-25s and CHF 500 for new drivers. It varies a lot by insurer: Zurich Insurance adds CHF 2,000 on top of the normal deductible on a collision claim caused by a driver under 25; Smile applies the young-driver deductible only if he is not the declared main driver; Allianz and Elvia have no forced young-driver deductible at all. Some insurers let you buy the deductible out for a surcharge.
  • Bonus-Malus entry is an insurer choice, and it matters as much as age. Allianz starts a new driver at 100% and drops one step per claim-free year (plus four steps per at-fault claim); Mobiliar starts under-26s at 65%; Zurich Insurance reduces the young-driver loading every year until 25 and refunds 10% after three claim-free years. One at-fault claim in year one is expensive twice: the deductible and the bonus jump.
  • Age dominates everything. Comparis (43,877 online quotes, January 2025) found a 20-year-old man in Zurich pays up to 145% more than a 42-year-old for the same car, and that the spread between the cheapest and dearest insurer for the same young driver and car is CHF 2,000 to 4,000 a year. Comparing insurers matters more for him than for anyone else.
  • Powerful cars: no Swiss insurer publishes a kW cut-off or a stated refusal policy for young drivers; power is simply a rating factor, and a 200 PS car prices at a multiple of a 100 PS one. What is documented: a leasing company (Gowago) refuses under-25s anything over 300 PS, and comparison data shows the "sporty" variant of the same model pushing a young driver's worst-case Vollkasko to over CHF 9,000. Refusals on a 130i or CLK 350 for a 20-year-old are plausible but not documented anywhere, so treat those two rows below as "only if a quote actually comes back at a sane number".
  • Hauptlenker must be him. The car can be registered on a parent (some insurers then give one bonus step for a parent who is already a customer, Zurich gives 15% if the parents hold two policies), but the policy must name Ben as the most frequent driver. Declaring a parent as main driver when Ben drives most is an Anzeigepflichtverletzung: the insurer can cancel the policy, refuse the payout and claw back what it already paid, and on a liability claim the recourse can follow the driver for years. Not worth it.
  • His job does not help. Premiums are rated on age, licence years, claims record, postcode, nationality, occupation and the car. No insurer page or AVB checked gives any credit for professional driving experience or garage employment; the garage's own policy covers customer cars at work and nothing else. A voluntary Fahrsicherheitskurs (CHF 200 to 400) does earn a discount at several insurers, and Mobiliar pays CHF 100 toward it.
  • Discounts that exist: Mobiliar CleverDrive, telematics app for 18 to 30 year olds, up to 20% in year one and 25% after. AXA Safe Driver Bonus, 15% for 18 to 25 in exchange for a prevention programme and consent for AXA to check licence withdrawals and read vehicle data after a crash (the old Crash Recorder box is gone). Smile has a pay-how-you-drive app. Direct insurers (Smile, Simpego, the online lines of the big names) are typically cheapest for young drivers; in a 2019 Comparis comparison the same under-25 Golf driver paid CHF 431 at AXA and CHF 770 at Zurich for liability. Paying yearly instead of monthly saves a few percent.

What to actually do

  • Haftpflicht + Teilkasko, accept the forced young-driver deductible on liability. Teilkasko covers theft, glass, hail, fire, animal collision, marten damage and vandalism, which is the damage he cannot prevent, and it is cheap. Vollkasko on a 13 to 20-year-old car pays only the replacement value (CHF 7,000 to 10,000) minus the deductible, several insurers do not even offer collision cover past the seventh year of a car's life (Zurich Insurance's standard terms stop at year 7, Teilkasko at year 15), and TCS's rule of thumb is full cover to year 4, Teilkasko to year 7, liability only after that. The honest question is whether he can absorb writing the car off himself; if yes, skip Vollkasko. If any of the purchase is financed the lender will insist on it, which is one more reason to pay cash.
  • Add Bonusschutz if it is cheap (one at-fault claim does not move the bonus), skip Parkschadenversicherung unless it is nearly free.
  • Get quotes for three cars before test-driving any of them: the C 200 Kompressor, the Fiesta ST and the one he really wants. Use comparis.ch or the "Versicherungsprämie berechnen" button on each autoscout24 ad, then Mobiliar (CleverDrive), AXA (Safe Driver Bonus), Smile and Simpego directly, because the telematics and direct-insurer tariffs do not always show in comparison engines. Five quotes per car is the right number given the spread.
  • Grobfahrlässigkeit: by law the insurer can cut Kasko payouts and must take recourse on liability payouts for gross negligence (Allianz's example: 25% of an CHF 80,000 claim). The Grobfahrlässigkeitsschutz add-on waives that, but every insurer excludes alcohol, drugs and speeding offences from the waiver. On a 0.1 promille licence, one beer voids it. Worth having for the honest-mistake cases anyway.
  • Quick total-cost sanity check for the C 200 Kompressor: liability plus Teilkasko about CHF 1,500, road tax CHF 320, fuel for a 40 km daily commute at 8 l/100 km about CHF 1,300, first-year maintenance reserve CHF 1,500: roughly CHF 4,600 a year before parking. The 130i on the same commute is nearer CHF 5,900 (insurance about 1,900, tax 520, fuel at 10 l/100 km about 1,650, reserve 1,500) with a real chance of no sensible quote at all, and it will still be worth about the same in three years, so the extra CHF 1,300 a year is pure running cost.
